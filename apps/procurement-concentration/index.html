Eighteen vendors, half the money

Ottawa committed $5.97 billion to contracts between 2023 and 2025, spread across 1,620 vendors. Eighteen of them took half of it.

The city publishes contract awards twice a year, as a fresh dataset each time, with the column names spelled differently in every release. Nine such releases exist. Stitched together they make a series nobody has published: 5,648 award actions, every vendor, every department, and the by-law clause cited whenever a contract skipped competition entirely.

Concentration is not itself a scandal — someone has to run the city's waste collection, and there are not many firms that can. But the shape of the curve is worth seeing, and what sits underneath it is more interesting than the curve.

One clause does most of the sole-sourcing

70% of all non-competitive value cites a single clause

1,081 awards — 19% of them, worth $911M — went out without competition, each citing a clause of Procurement By-law s.22(1). Eleven different clauses appear. One of them, clause (D), accounts for $633M across 536 awards: seventy cents of every non-competitive dollar.

Thirteen firms that never compete

Of the 163 vendors paid more than $5M, thirteen received essentially all of it without competition — $632M between them. The seven largest are below. For some the reasoning is self-evident: nobody else sells Microsoft licences.

The largest is not a software licence. Envari Energy Solutions is the single biggest recipient of sole-sourced money in the city — $240M over nine awards, every one under clause (D), for professional engineering services. That is more than Microsoft and SAP combined, under the same clause used to renew their licences.

A quarter of the money is added after the award

$1.48B in amendments and extensions

664 contracts — 14% of them — were amended or extended at least once. Those top-ups total $1.48B, or a quarter of everything committed: money attached to work already under way rather than put to the market. One Bell Mobility contract appears eleven times across three reporting periods, growing each time.

How this was made

Nine half-year releases from Open Ottawa, normalised into one table. Each release is the same schema with different column spellings — Amount in one, F_Amount_ in another — so the loader maps whatever arrived onto a stable set of fields rather than a hard-coded list, and the next drop needs no edit.

A row is an award action, not a contract: an initial award, an amendment or an extension. A contract amended twice appears three times. Vendor totals count actions; the contract file deduplicates. Vendor names are used as published with no entity resolution, so a firm spelled two ways is counted twice — which understates concentration rather than overstating it.

Coverage is uneven at the edges: 2022 is Transit Commission only, and the all-departments series begins in 2023. Two reporting streams run in parallel and are near-disjoint; 14 duplicate rows across the whole set were dropped.
